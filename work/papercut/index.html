Our Story

It started with a kitchen table and a very brave pair of safety scissors.

In 2023, Marisol and Dev — a paper artist and a primary-school teacher — grew tired of craft kits that were all plastic stick-ons and none craft. So every month they designed one paper world, pre-cut every fiddly piece on their kitchen table, and put it in a kraft box for the twelve kids on their street.

Three years on, the kitchen table is a studio in Asheville, but the promise is identical: 100% paper, every piece either pre-cut or cuttable with safety scissors, and steps drawn so clearly that a 4-year-old can lead and a grown-up just holds the glue.

Sheet 1 — Inside

What is actually in the box

Four things, always. No Tony-bag-of-fillers, no loose glitter that lives in your carpet until 2036. Just paper, tools, glue and a card.

Pre-Cut Paper Pieces

Between 22 and 40 pieces, die-cut from 160gsm FSC paper in up to 12 colors per scene. Circles, scallops, tabs and fold lines are all done — small hands just pop and fold.

Right-Sized Safety Tools

Blunt-nose scissors with a real spring assist for ages 4–6, Proper snips for 7+, two glue-dot sheets, and a paper-safe bone folder shaped like a fat crayon. Zero extra purchases.

Illustrated Step Cards

One laminated card per scene: 6 numbered steps, one drawing each, no more than 12 words per step. Both parents and 4-year-olds rate it clearly enough to do unaided.

Sheet 3 — How It Works

Cut. Fold. Paste. Repeat next month.

The kit changed in three years; the ritual hasn't. Most families run it end to end on a rainy Saturday.

1
Step 1 — CUT

Pop the pieces out

The tray is ordered by step, not by part number. Each piece lifts clean from its sheet; the fold lines are pre-scored so nothing splits.

2
Step 2 — FOLD

Follow the big arrows

Mountain folds to the front, valley folds to the back, drawn exactly as they'll look when finished. The bone-folder crayon creases edges sharp without tearing.

3
Step 3 — PASTE

Glue dots, no mess

One glue-dot sheet per scene, sized per joint. By the last step the scene stands, hangs or frames itself — and it's ready to mail to Grandma.
