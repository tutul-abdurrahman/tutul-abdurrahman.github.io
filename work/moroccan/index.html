Riad & hammam · Marrakech medina

Behind a cedar door, a courtyard of oranges and lantern light.

Dar Zellij is a restored 19th-century riad of nine rooms, a calm hammam and a rooftop facing the Koutoubia. Hand-cut zellige, warm tadelakt and brasswork by the medina's last five workshops — nothing here is mass-produced.

An invitation

Intricacy is how a house says welcome.

Moroccan houses have always spoken through their surfaces: a star carved where a hand rests, a tile cut the week a guest is expected so the pattern is fresh under their palm. Dar Zellij keeps that language. Every tile was cut and set by craftsmen from the Derb el-Horra quarter; every lantern was hammered by the Amrani brothers, not bought finished. The geometry you will run your fingers along took eleven months to return to the walls — and it will outlast all of us.

The three rooms of the house

Courtyard, hammam,
and the rooftop

The riad is organised as a journey from the street's heat to the water's cool to the sky. These are the three spaces guests live inside.

The orange courtyard

Nine bitter-orange trees around a marble fountain, arcades of hand-cut zellige on two sides and a four-metre horseshoe arch framed in carved cedar. Breakfast is served under the bougainvillea at any hour you choose.

The calm hammam

A traditional steam room in green tadelakt, heated by the old beldi firewood system, with three temperature rooms and the kessa glove ritual. Named the best hammam in the medina by Bon Voyage Algérie in 2025.

The ritual, step by step

The Koutoubia rooftop

Two terraces, twelve brass lanterns lit at dusk, and the call to prayer arriving across the rooftops at eye level. Sunset dinner is prepared by our chef Fatima Zahra Benkirane and her three-woman kitchen.

Why guests choose the dar

A riad that behaves like a large house, not a hotel

Every room is different, deliberately

Nine rooms, nine protagonists: the Star Suite with its 8-point zellige ceiling medallion, the Saffron Room with a mashrabiya balcony over the courtyard, the Cedar Room with the original 1843 doors.

Staff kept like family, house-manager first

Karim the house manager answers his own phone from 8am. Nothing is too small: the cobbler, the pharmacy, the cousin's carpet shop — the medina opens through him.

Tarifs are final, with the taxes folded in

Rates include the 2.5% tourist tax, hammam session, breakfast and the airport pickup. We have not added a single supplement since reopening in 2024.

Craft money stays in the medina

Five workshops — zellige, tadelakt, brass, cedar and plaster — keep the house alive between guests, and guests may sit at their benches for an afternoon by arrangement.

Your days, three rhythms

From mint tea to lantern light

A stay at Dar Zellij follows the medina's own clock. Three movements, each with its own people who look after it.

Movement 1
1

Morning — the medina wakes

Tea on the courtyard at 7:30, then Karim walks you into the souks before the crowds: the coppersmiths at 8, the dyers' alley by 9, back home while the tour groups queue for the same lanes at noon.

Movement 2
2

Afternoon — steam & shade

The hammam warms at 2pm. One hour of steam and the kessa ritual, then mint tea on the lounging terrace. The fountain does the rest — guests call it "the hour the riad hums".

Movement 3
3

Evening — lantern hour

At dusk the roof terrace lights twelve brass lanterns and the fire is lit for Fatima's tagine — lamb with preserved quince on Thursdays, sea bream pastilla on Saturdays. Booking is one phone call from any room.

18 Apr 2026 · Zellige

Why we count the tiles twice

Twenty-one thousand four hundred tiles came back to the walls between 2024 and 2025. Maâlem Hakim's rule: score twice, cut once — and a star is never rejected, only resold last.

Read the note
2 Mar 2026 · Brass

Sixty lanterns, one workshop

All sixty-three lanterns in the riad — roof, arcade and hammam — are made by the Amrani brothers, fourth-generation coppersmiths two streets away. This is how a lamp grows for six weeks.

Read the note
14 Feb 2026 · Tadelakt

The tadelakt is polished with a stone

Lime plaster from the Ourika road, olive soap, and a river pebble worked in circles for hours. Here is how the bathroom walls stay sealed and warm to the touch — no paint, no grout, no shortcuts.
