A declaration

We perfume the way a garden breathes at dusk

Verdure was founded by a Lyonnais perfumer, Hélène Rouzier, in a courtyard workshop where the ivy was left to climb through the window frame. It has stayed there since — through three houses, two wars and one flood. Our rule has not moved: nothing enters a composition that did not grow, and nothing leaves the atelier before the fortieth day. Perfume is not a product we ship; it is a statement we stand behind, drawn annually, in ink that fades only if the wearer stops imagining.

The advantages

What the house promises its wearers

  • Nothing that did not grow

    Every material in the house is plant-derived. No synthetics, no isolates, no exceptions since 1953.

  • The fortieth day rule

    Four weeks of maceration minimum, three of rest before bottling. Perfume is patience, written as a scheduled hour.

  • Editions, not inventory

    Yearly compositions run while the harvest holds. When your bottle is finished, it is finished — and recorded in the house registry.

  • Scent in prose

    Every composition ships with a letter describing its notes and its picking day — the same note the perfumer wrote before it was blended.

“A perfume should be signed like a drawing — one line, one breath, and the light goes down at the right moment.”
Hélène Rouzier, 1931 — house register, page 12
The composition

From plot to pomade, three movements

01
Picking day — March

The harvest

Botanicals arrive the same day as picking, weighed in grams against 1927 house registers. Anything wilted is composted at the gate — never blended.

02
Forty days — quieter

The maceration

Solids steep in cold-pressed carrier under amber glass, funnelled daily at first light. No vibration, no urgency, no tasting notes for customers who ask.

03
Bottling — 17th of May

The bottling

Arch-top vessels are filled by hand, corked with cotton and bee's wax, sealed with the leaf crest, and logged against edition counts in the registry ledger.

Asked of the house

Questions & registers

It does not take forty days to fill a bottle — it takes forty days to make the perfume that fills it. Maceration is measured from the day the last botanical enters the steeping vessel. The fortieth date is printed on your letter, not promised in an email.

Retirement happens when a harvest changes character and the composition would become a recreation instead of a drawing. Registry wearers are offered remaining stock first, in order of registration date. Retired edition letters stay on the shelf in the reading room at the atelier.

The atelier on rue des Iris is open Thursdays from two until seven, without appointment. You will smell the blotter library, see the maceration shelf, and probably be handed a chair by the ivy window. Triptych wearers visit annually with one guest.

Since 1953 — the year Hélène Rouzier's successor Fanny Declercq refused an isolate lot — no synthetic has entered the house. The full botanical index for each composition is printed on its letter and reads like a grocery list from a very determined garden.
